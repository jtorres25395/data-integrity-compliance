# IV — Conclusion

## Why I Started This Project

This project began with a question about **Guardian**.

After Guardian replaced CHILDS, public reporting began describing problems involving foster-parent payments, case information, system workflow, and other functions dependent on accurate child-welfare data. Those failures looked familiar to me because I had already seen how missing placements, authorization problems, and payment problems could develop while working inside DCS.

My original question was simple:

**Were Guardian's payment problems and placement-data problems connected to an operational weakness that already existed?**

The difficulty was that no available public dataset directly answered that question.

## Building a Way to Test Success and Failure

DCS semiannual placement tables presented an interesting problem. Every placement category could add to 100 percent. From a reporting standpoint, that suggests complete data.

But a table totaling 100 percent does not tell us **why anything happened**.

It cannot tell us why Relative Foster Home Placement increased, why Family Foster declined, why a child appeared in No Identified Placement (NIP), or why one reporting period performed better than another.

I therefore used the available categories as **proxies**.

A stronger historical period was established as a baseline. Relative, Family Foster, Independent Living, Group Home, Shelter, NIP, Runaway/Absconded, and later Missing Child were examined together for patterns. Those signals were then tested against contemporaneous news reports, government reports, audits, and other independent evidence.

The purpose was not to claim individual child movement that the public data cannot prove.

The purpose was to answer a different question:

**Can the available data tell us when the placement system appears to be working—and when it appears to be under pressure?**

The validation showed that it can.

That created something the existing reporting framework did not provide: **a method for testing program success and failure rather than merely reporting activity.**

## What Success Looked Like

The stronger periods in the placement tables were not produced by a computer acting alone.

I saw part of the human system behind those results while working in Pima County.

Centralized Referral and IPS staff specialized in the administrative machinery behind child-welfare work: placements, authorizations, supervised visitation, missing-placement problems, and additional supports available to relatives caring for children.

They were not case managers trying to perform this work between investigations and field responsibilities.

**This was their job.**

The IPS function described here is based partly on my firsthand operational experience. I have not located a statewide DCS report documenting the Pima IPS function in the same detail. However, historical Arizona employment records show the IPS classification existed, while I have not found a comparable current DCS position publicly identified under that title.

The public record is much clearer about another specialized role: **Placement Coordinators**.

Arizona DCS documented the development and use of Placement Coordinator positions, including work involving relative searches and placement support.

[Arizona DCS — FFY 2017 Annual Progress and Services Report](https://dcs.az.gov/sites/default/files/DCS-Reports/FFY-2017-Arizona-APSR.PDF)

DCS also documented specialized kinship-support functions. In Pima County, the **Kinship Liaison Support Unit** helped kinship families obtain resources and assistance needed to maintain placements.

[Arizona DCS — FY2018 Supportive Resources Annual Report](https://dcs.az.gov/sites/default/files/DCS-Reports/DCS%20Supportive%20Resources%20Annual%20Report_FY2018.pdf)

Arizona court records from 2018 also describe DCS moving toward centralized placement administration, developing kinship functions, moving children away from congregate care, and reporting progress reducing shelter use.

[Arizona Courts — Commission on Juvenile Justice Meeting Minutes, January 2018](https://www.azcourts.gov/Portals/0/29/JJSD%20Meetings/COJC/Minutes01_25_18.pdf)

These reports independently support the operating model I observed.

## The Madera Welcome Center

I saw what specialization looked like at the **Madera Welcome Center**.

The placement staff talked directly with children, particularly teenagers. They learned about them, worked with providers and shelters, and aggressively searched for an appropriate placement.

The goal was not to become efficient at operating a Welcome Center.

**The goal was to keep it empty.**

That distinction is important.

The placement function also expanded into work with relatives and kinship caregivers. Staff developed relationships with families, helped them obtain needed resources, addressed barriers, and encouraged appropriate relatives to complete foster-home licensing.

This was not my program design.

**Relative-to-Family-Foster was DCS's strategy.**

My question was whether we could develop a method capable of showing when that strategy worked, when it failed, and why.

## The Welcome Center Contradiction

The public record provides an unusually clear example of how the operating philosophy changed.

In 2015–2016, Phoenix's emergency placement center was described as a **last resort** for children who could not immediately be placed. The facility agreement limited stays to 23 hours, and DCS leadership said children should be placed within approximately 24 hours. DCS was also increasing specialized emergency-placement staffing so children could move into foster or kinship placements more quickly.

[Phoenix New Times — Emergency Placement Center, 2016](https://www.phoenixnewtimes.com/news/as-phoenix-s-emergency-shelter-for-kids-closes-children-will-sleep-in-dcs-offices-again-7999251/)

That same reporting contained an important warning: workers said a facility intended as a **last resort** was already becoming a **first resort** when the placement system became overwhelmed.

By 2023, DCS was openly using different language.

The new Phoenix Welcome Center was officially described by DCS as a child's **"first stop"** when entering foster care.

[Arizona DCS — New Welcome Center, 2023](https://dcs.az.gov/news/dcs-launches-new-trauma-informed-center-kids-entering-foster-care)

That is not a small language change.

**Earlier model:** the emergency setting represented a placement problem that specialized people were expected to resolve quickly.

**Current model:** the emergency setting has become an incorporated step in the placement pathway itself.

In Pima County, I had seen specialized workers succeed at the earlier objective.

## Then Guardian Arrived

Guardian was supposed to improve upon CHILDS.

CHILDS had legitimate weaknesses involving usability, mobile access, reporting, and data quality. Guardian's approved development budget was approximately **$86.1 million**, and the replacement was intended to address those weaknesses and better support DCS operations.

[Arizona Auditor General — Report 23-104](https://www.azauditor.gov/sites/default/files/2023-11/23-104_Report.pdf)

But Guardian did not simply eliminate the old problems.

Auditors documented programming and migration errors that resulted in **underpayments and overpayments to foster and adoptive caregivers**.

They also identified problems involving:

* data quality;
* timely and accurate reporting;
* highly segmented workflows;
* missing critical information;
* difficulty correcting information;
* hundreds of outstanding user requests.

Guardian's problems also reached child-safety monitoring.

After the system launched in February 2021, DCS suspended its biweekly reviews of children missing from care from **February through July 2021** while it developed the Guardian reporting necessary to conduct those reviews.

[Arizona Auditor General — Report 21-113](https://www.azauditor.gov/sites/default/files/2023-11/21-113_Report.pdf)

## The Problem Reached the Courts

The court is one of the most important outside controls in the child-welfare system.

Judges determine whether children remain in state care, return home, or move toward another permanent outcome. Those decisions depend on accurate and timely information from DCS.

A 2023 audit found that **46 of 67 sampled court reports—69 percent—were not submitted within the required timeframe**.

Judges also reported problems with the quality of information they received.

The same audit found approximately **96,000 provider documents in Guardian that had not been incorporated into children's official case records**. Nearly 1,900 of those documents were associated with **596 open juvenile court cases**.

That means caseworkers may not have known about information that should have been considered when preparing reports for the courts.

[Arizona Auditor General — Report 23-115](https://www.azauditor.gov/sites/default/files/2023-11/23-115_Report.pdf)

Some court-reporting problems existed before Guardian. Guardian cannot fairly be blamed for every weakness in Arizona child welfare.

But that makes the central question more important, not less.

**Guardian was supposed to improve the weaknesses of the system it replaced.**

Corrective work related to Guardian case-document problems was still being tracked by the Auditor General in 2025.

[Arizona Auditor General — 18-Month Follow-Up](https://www.azauditor.gov/sites/default/files/2025-10/23-115_18-mo_Followup.pdf)

## The Missing Piece Was People

This project does not conclude that CHILDS was a perfect computer system.

It was not.

What it does show is something more important:

**CHILDS was never just software. CHILDS was people using software.**

Specialized workers knew when an authorization did not make sense.

They knew when placement information needed cleanup.

They knew when a relative needed additional support.

They could talk with a teenager and understand why one placement might succeed where another would fail.

They could recognize when the computer record did not match what was actually happening in the field.

The stronger placement periods occurred while these specialized functions were clearly part of the operating environment.

Later, several of those functions either became harder to identify publicly, changed form, or were absorbed into a very different operating model.

That produces a two-part problem:

**the technology changed, and the human system around the technology changed with it.**

The placement tables alone cannot prove that losing or changing specialized staff caused Guardian-era deterioration.

But the combination of the placement signals, historical staffing evidence, Welcome Center history, Guardian audits, payment failures, court-reporting problems, and case-record problems provides enough evidence to ask the question seriously:

**When DCS replaced CHILDS, did it preserve the specialized human capabilities that had made CHILDS work?**

## Technology Is an Aid, Not the Path

This project also reinforced something larger about technology.

Technology can calculate faster, search more information, recognize patterns, and make capable workers dramatically more productive.

AI helped me do exactly that during this project.

But AI did not decide that missing placements, foster payments, relative licensing, recruitment funding, Welcome Centers, Guardian, and historical staffing might belong in the same investigation.

A person had to recognize that path.

A computer works from the information, rules, patterns, and methods available to it.

Experienced people can recognize when those methods no longer explain reality, question the assumptions, and create another way forward.

**Technology can aid expertise. It cannot substitute for the human judgment that determines where the investigation should go next.**

## How Success Should Be Measured

The solution is not simply another program, another report, or another increase in funding.

DCS already created the Relative-to-Family-Foster pathway.

The problem is determining whether it actually works.

That requires tracking the pathway itself:

**Relative identified → supports provided → barriers addressed → licensing initiated → licensing completed → stable Family Foster placement or another permanent family outcome**

At each stage, DCS should be able to measure:

* time in each stage;
* placement disruption;
* unresolved placement status;
* emergency-setting use;
* licensing delays;
* authorization problems;
* payment problems;
* reasons the pathway continued;
* reasons the pathway stopped.

That changes the analytical question from:

**How many children were in each category?**

to:

**What made this pathway succeed, where did it fail, and what intervention changed the result?**

That is the difference between reporting activity and managing performance.

## What Comes Next

The proxy-and-validation method developed in this project can now be tested against another part of the child-welfare system: **exits from care**.

Reunification, adoption, guardianship, aging out, and other exits create a second 100-percent dataset.

If the same analytical method can successfully identify meaningful patterns and validate them against contemporaneous evidence there, then this project may have developed something broader than a placement-analysis technique.

It may provide a better way to examine the entire child-welfare pathway when continuous child-level public data is unavailable.

## Final Finding

DCS already demonstrated that specialized people, family-placement strategies, and operational troubleshooting could produce stronger periods.

The challenge is not recreating the past simply because it was the past.

The challenge is identifying **why something worked, preserving the parts that produced success, continuously measuring them, and changing the parts that do not.**

Guardian was built to make Arizona child welfare better.

**Where is the measurable evidence that it did?**
