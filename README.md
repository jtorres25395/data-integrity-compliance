# Operational Data Integrity and Compliance

## Project Overview

Arizona’s child-welfare placement data can appear stable while important operational activity remains difficult to see. This portfolio examines one central question:

**How do I make Relative Foster Home Placement more successful through better tracking methods that reveal what works and what doesn’t?**

The project began with a problem I already knew operationally: missing placement information. In earlier DCS work, a missing-placement list was reviewed and corrected every six months. When I later examined the public placement reports, I expected to find a comparable measure.

I did not.

That became the investigative lead.

## The Reporting Problem

[AFCARS Technical Bulletin #4 – Data Compliance](https://www.acf.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/documents/cb/afcars_tb4.pdf) established limits for missing, invalid, and internally inconsistent data. A data file could therefore meet reporting requirements while still containing errors below the federal compliance threshold.

At first, the public data looked strong. Placement categories reconciled to the reported Out-of-Home total, and several analytical tests produced reasonable results.

Then the limitation became clear.

The DCS Semi-Annual Child Welfare Reports are snapshots. They show where children were reported at a point in time, but they cannot reconstruct a continuous child-level history of every placement, disruption, move, temporary setting, or unresolved record occurring during the six-month period.

The numbers could reconcile without showing the entire operational process.

## Following the Missing Placement Signal

We were using DCS Semi-Annual Child Welfare Reports as the primary public dataset. I expected to find a public measure comparable to the missing-placement list I had worked internally. When it was not there, we did not create a replacement number and call it evidence.

We looked for its operational footprint.

In 2016, [*As Phoenix’s Emergency Shelter for Kids Closes, Children Will Sleep in DCS Offices Again*](https://www.phoenixnewtimes.com/news/as-phoenix-s-emergency-shelter-for-kids-closes-children-will-sleep-in-dcs-offices-again-7999251/) documented children using an emergency placement center while DCS searched for foster placements.

The [April–September 2016 Semi-Annual Child Welfare Report](https://dcs.az.gov/sites/default/files/DCS-Reports/SEMIANNUAL%20REPORTING%20REQUIREMENTS%2004-01%20through%2009-30%20_FINAL.pdf) separately reported children remaining in **Shelter >21 Days**.

Then another clue appeared. A 2017 report, [*DCS Questions Remain, Is Arizona's Child Welfare Agency Really Improving?*](https://arizonadailyindependent.com/2017/05/16/questions-about-dcs-mckays-qualifications-claims-persist/), reported **494 children in No Identified Placement (NIP) in March 2017**.

The public category existed. What remained difficult to see was the operational history behind it.

Years later, DCS itself documented another temporary setting. In [*DCS Launches New Trauma-Informed Center for Kids Entering Foster Care*](https://dcs.az.gov/news/dcs-launches-new-trauma-informed-center-kids-entering-foster-care), the Department reported that approximately **212 children per month** entered the Maricopa Welcome Center, with an **average stay of 48 hours**, and that approximately **27% of children entering care** used the center.

These settings and conditions were measurable.

The question changed from looking for one missing number to asking what the public placement data could—and could not—show about what happened between reporting points.

## Family Foster Bottleneck

The strongest recurring pattern centered on family foster placement capacity.

Payment problems, placement disruptions, group-home pressure, recruitment efforts, unresolved placement records, and changes in family-based placement repeatedly brought the analysis back to whether the system could identify, support, and retain stable family foster placements.

The **2021 Guardian transition** became an important stress point. [*Some Arizona Foster and Adoptive Parents Experience Payment Delays*](https://ktar.com/arizona-news/some-arizona-foster-and-adoptive-parents-experience-payment-delays) documented payment problems after Guardian went live. DCS acknowledged system glitches, and approximately 200 foster families caring for children for partial months were reported as affected.

The placement data also showed a substantial break from the relative stability visible in earlier years.

This does not establish that Guardian caused every later placement problem. It shows why placement records, authorization, payment, disruption, and family foster capacity should not be examined as isolated processes.

The same placement-information problem kept appearing in different forms.

## What the Investigation Changed

The investigation began by looking for a missing-placement count.

It eventually became a measurement problem.

No Identified Placement (NIP), Runaway/Absconded, Shelter >21 Days, Missing Child, Family Foster placement, funding patterns, and contemporaneous evidence each provide different signals. None can independently reconstruct a child’s complete placement history.

The portfolio therefore does not treat proxy measures as proof. It uses them to identify patterns, test explanations, and determine where better tracking is needed.

Federal AFCARS reporting also evolved. [AFCARS 2020 Technical Bulletin #20](https://acf.gov/cb/training-technical-assistance/technical-bulletin-20-data-adoption) expanded reporting requirements for out-of-home care, including detailed living-arrangement information.

That moved the reporting model closer to the question this project had already reached:

**What happened to the child between reporting points?**

## Continuous Tracking Solution

The proposed solution is not another snapshot.

A continuous internal model should follow placement activity throughout the reporting period: initial placement, additional placements, disruptions, replacement activity, unresolved records, authorization, and related operational changes.

Public reporting can then summarize that activity through privacy-safe measures while the underlying system preserves the continuous history needed for operational analysis.

This creates a way to measure more than whether the reporting process was completed. It allows stakeholders to identify what strengthens Relative Foster Home Placement, what destabilizes it, and where intervention is needed.

Without mindful attention to missing placement information, the same problems can be corrected temporarily and still return in another form.

The purpose of this portfolio is to make those problems measurable while they are happening.
